Project 1

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection

Reassembling Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii's glass-plate negatives into color images, automatically.

1

A camera that took three pictures at once

In 1907 Prokudin-Gorskii set out to photograph the Russian Empire in color. There was just one issue: this was decades before the invention of color film. Prokudin-Gorskii the idea to shoot each scene three times. Once with a red filter, once with green, and once with blue. All of these were recorded onto a single glass plate. The scanned negatives are availible online here.

Constructing these negatives into a full-color image seems trivial. Split the plate into even thirds making channels of blue, green and red (top to bottom). Stacking these channels should should reveal the photograph. It does not. In order to take the photos, the plates and filters had to be changed, which causes some movement of the camera. Not to mention, scenes are live, trees can sway in the wind, people can move around. The naieve approach of stacking the channels leads to poor images. So the challenge of this project comes down to 2 numbers for green and red: the (x, y) shift that aligns them with blue, which stays fixed as the reference.

2

Scoring an alignment: L2 and NCC

To search for an offset I first need to answer a smaller question: given a candidate shift, how good is it? Two metrics, both computed over the two channels as flat vectors:

L2 / sum of squared differences. sum((A - B)**2), and lower is better. It asks how close the two channels are pixel for pixel. This is cheap, easy, and works well when the two channels have similar brightness.

Normalized cross-correlation. Normalize each channel to zero mean and unit norm, then take the dot product; higher is better. Since the mean is subtracted and the vector is rescaled, NCC measures whether the two channels have the same structure (edges and texture in the same places, regardless of brightness differences) This is vital when comparing channels, since each filter allows different amounts of light. In light of this fact, I did not bother with L2 as my evaluation metric.

Advice: crop before you score

Every plate has junk at the edges, which can cause probems since the shift is implemented with np.roll, which is circular. This causes the junk from the bottom to be added to the top, which can give you worse scores than you should have. To combat this, I cropped 10% of each image before evaluationg, which got rid of the majority of noise and artifacts from the medium of photography. This led to much better results.

3

Single-scale

On the small JPEGs the simple approach suffices. I take every candidate displacement in a window of ±50 pixels in each direction, apply it, score the cropped overlap with NCC, and keep the winner. That is 101 × 101, which comes out to 10,200 candidate shifts per channel, and each one must be scored. This is permissible when the image is 390 × 341.

4

Pyramid: Solution to scaling

The full-resolution plates are around 3500 pixels wide, roughly 80 × the pixel count of the JPEGs, and their displacements are orders of magnitude larger. Exhaustive search suffers in 2 directions: the search window has to grow to ±200 or so, which is about 160,000 candidates instead of 10,200, and each candidate now scores 80 × as many pixels. That product is the difference between four seconds and tens of minutes (and sometimes more). I have a 10-minute run of one melons alignment to prove the point.

A coarse-to-fine image pyramid removes almost all of that work. Repeatedly downsample the image by half until it is much smaller; a 180-pixel displacement at full resolution is a 5-pixel displacement five levels down. My algorithm:

Base case: This case is hit at the coarsest level, where it runs the full exhaustive search. The image is about 110 pixels wide, so even a wide window is nearly free.
Recursive case: take the offset found one level coarser, double it (this works since the image just doubled in size), apply it, and search only a small (±2) window around it for a residual correction. Each level refines the estimate by one pixel of precision at that level's scale.

5

Results: the provided plates

All offsets are the displacement applied to that channel to register it onto blue, given as (x, y). Blue is the fixed reference, so its offset will always be (0, 0), by construction.

6

Results: my own picks from the collection

Three more plates pulled from the Library of Congress collection and run through the same pyramid.

7

Where I struggled

Emir is the standard hard case, and it caused problems in my pipeline. At 5 pyramid levels the search locks onto a badly wrong offset for red and the reconstruction comes out as a ghost. A second, red Emir is visible.

I believe this is caused by brightness, as he is rather still. The Emir's robe is a saturated blue: it is very bright in the blue channel and nearly black in the red one. At the coarsest pyramid level, where the whole robe is a handful of pixels, there is little structure left between the two channels for the metric to latch onto.

My solution was to run this with 2 levels instead of 5. The coarsest level is then large enough to still contain real edge structure, and NCC finds the true offset. The cost is speed: 16.6 seconds instead of 4.6, because the exhaustive base-case search now runs on a much bigger image.
